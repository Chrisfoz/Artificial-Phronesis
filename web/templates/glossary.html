Wisdom Glossary

Key terms and concepts from wisdom research

Core Concepts

Wisdom vs Intelligence
Core Concept
Intelligence refers to cognitive abilities—pattern recognition, problem-solving, learning speed. Wisdom involves the application of knowledge and experience with good judgment, particularly in complex, uncertain, or value-laden situations. Intelligence asks "Can this be done?" while wisdom asks "Should this be done, and for whom?"
Artificial Wisdom
Core Concept
A framework for developing AI systems that support wiser human judgment rather than merely optimizing for capability. Artificial Wisdom emphasizes uncertainty acknowledgment, value trade-offs, perspective-taking, and reflective balance over speed and confidence.
Related: Wisdom vs Intelligence
Wise Reasoning
Core Concept
A cognitive process characterized by intellectual humility, recognition of uncertainty, consideration of multiple perspectives, and integration of different viewpoints. Developed by Igor Grossmann, wise reasoning is measurable and can be cultivated through specific practices.

SD-WISE Traits

Self-Reflection (Self-Insight)
SD-WISE Trait
The capacity to examine one's own thoughts, feelings, motivations, and behaviors with honesty and depth. Self-reflective individuals regularly question their assumptions and seek to understand the deeper reasons behind their actions.
Prosocial Behaviors (Compassion/Altruism)
SD-WISE Trait
Actions intended to benefit others, driven by genuine concern for their wellbeing. Includes empathy, compassion, and willingness to help even at personal cost. A key component of wisdom that connects individual insight to social benefit.
Emotional Regulation (Emotional Homeostasis)
SD-WISE Trait
The ability to manage and modulate emotional responses appropriately. Not suppression of emotions, but the capacity to experience emotions without being overwhelmed, and to return to equilibrium after disturbance.
Acceptance of Diverse Perspectives (Value Relativism)
SD-WISE Trait
Recognition that different people may hold different values and that these differences can be legitimate. Not moral relativism (anything goes), but acknowledgment that context matters and that reasonable people can disagree on complex issues.
Decisiveness
SD-WISE Trait
The ability to make timely decisions even under uncertainty. Wise decisiveness balances thorough consideration with the recognition that perfect information is rarely available and that inaction itself has consequences.
Social Advising (Pragmatic Knowledge of Life)
SD-WISE Trait
Practical wisdom about navigating life's challenges, including relationships, career, and personal development. The ability to offer sound guidance based on experience and understanding of human nature.
Spirituality (Self-Transcendence)
SD-WISE Trait
A sense of connection to something larger than oneself—whether understood religiously, philosophically, or through connection to nature, humanity, or future generations. Involves reflection on meaning and purpose.
Openness to New Experiences
SD-WISE Trait
Willingness to engage with novel ideas, perspectives, and situations. Includes intellectual curiosity, appreciation for learning, and comfort with ambiguity. Essential for continued growth and adaptation.
Sense of Humor
SD-WISE Trait
The ability to find lightness and perspective even in difficult situations. Not dismissiveness, but a capacity to maintain balance and not take oneself too seriously. Contributes to resilience and social connection.

Philosophical Concepts

Phronesis (Practical Wisdom)
Philosophy
An Aristotelian concept referring to practical wisdom—the ability to discern the right course of action in particular circumstances. Unlike theoretical knowledge (episteme) or technical skill (techne), phronesis involves judgment about what is good and how to achieve it in specific situations.
Eudaimonia
Philosophy
Often translated as "flourishing" or "wellbeing," eudaimonia in Aristotelian philosophy refers to living well and doing well—a life of virtue and meaning rather than mere pleasure or material success. Wisdom is considered essential for achieving eudaimonia.
Epistemic Humility
Philosophy
Recognition of the limits of one's knowledge and the possibility of being wrong. A key component of wise reasoning that guards against overconfidence and promotes openness to new information and perspectives.

Research Frameworks

SD-WISE (San Diego Wisdom Scale)
Framework
A validated psychometric instrument developed by Dilip Jeste and colleagues measuring nine components of wisdom: self-reflection, prosocial behaviors, emotional regulation, acceptance of diverse perspectives, decisiveness, social advising, spirituality, openness to new experiences, and sense of humor.
Berlin Wisdom Paradigm
Framework
Developed by Paul Baltes and colleagues at the Max Planck Institute, this framework defines wisdom as "expert knowledge in the fundamental pragmatics of life." It emphasizes factual knowledge, procedural knowledge, lifespan contextualism, value relativism, and recognition of uncertainty.
Three-Dimensional Wisdom Scale (3D-WS)
Framework
Monika Ardelt's model conceptualizing wisdom as the integration of cognitive (understanding life), reflective (self-examination and perspective-taking), and affective (compassion and sympathy) dimensions.
HERO(E) Model (Webster)
Framework
Jeffrey Dean Webster's Self-Assessed Wisdom Scale measuring five dimensions: Humor, Experience (critical life experiences), Reminiscence/Reflectiveness, Openness, and Emotional Regulation. Emphasizes the role of life experience in wisdom development.
